# Lumpsum Calculator – One-Time Investment Growth Guide

A **lumpsum investment** involves depositing a single, one-time sum of money into mutual funds, stocks, or other financial assets — rather than spreading contributions across regular monthly installments. It's a common choice for deploying windfalls: work bonuses, inheritances, or the proceeds from selling another asset.

This calculator projects your **Maturity Value** and **Estimated Wealth Gain** based on your principal, an assumed annual return rate, and your investment tenure.

## How One-Time Lumpsum Compounding Works

When you invest a lump sum, the entire amount starts earning returns immediately — there's no waiting for future contributions to arrive and begin compounding, unlike a SIP. Each year, growth is calculated on the full current balance (original principal plus everything it has already earned), which is why the gains accelerate the longer the money stays invested.

## The Lumpsum Formula

$$A = P \times (1 + r)^t$$

Where:
* **A**: Estimated maturity amount.
* **P**: Initial lumpsum principal.
* **r**: Expected annual rate of return, as a decimal (annual percentage ÷ 100).
* **t**: Investment tenure in years.

## Worked Example

For a lumpsum of **$50,000** invested for **10 years** at an assumed annual return of **12%**:

1. $r = 0.12$, $t = 10$
2. $A = 50{,}000 \times (1.12)^{10} = 50{,}000 \times 3.105848 \approx \$155{,}292.41$
3. Estimated wealth gain: $\$155{,}292.41 - \$50{,}000.00 = \$105{,}292.41$

## Tenure Sensitivity ($50,000 Lumpsum @ 12% p.a.)

| Tenure | Maturity Value | Estimated Wealth Gain |
| :--- | :---: | :---: |
| 5 years | $88,117.08 | $38,117.08 |
| 10 years (example above) | $155,292.41 | $105,292.41 |
| 15 years | $273,678.29 | $223,678.29 |
| 20 years | $482,314.65 | $432,314.65 |

*Doubling the tenure from 10 to 20 years more than triples the maturity value — a direct result of compounding acting on an already-larger balance each year.*

## Lumpsum vs. SIP

A lumpsum investment puts 100% of your capital to work immediately, which gives it the maximum possible time to compound and tends to produce stronger results when markets trend upward from the moment you invest. The tradeoff is timing risk: if the market drops shortly after you invest, your entire principal is exposed to that decline at once. A SIP instead spreads the same total capital across many purchase dates, which softens the impact of short-term downturns but also means less money is invested — and compounding — in the early months. Neither approach is universally better; the right one depends on how much capital you have available at once, your risk tolerance, and your outlook on near-term market direction.
