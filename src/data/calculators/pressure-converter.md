### Pressure: Force Per Unit Area

Pressure measures how much force is applied over a given area. Push a nail with your thumb and the sharp point concentrates all that force into a tiny area — high pressure. Push with your palm and the same force is spread across a much larger area — low pressure. Mathematically: Pressure = Force / Area.

This concept appears everywhere: the air pushing against every surface (atmospheric pressure), gas compressed in a car tire, steam in a boiler, water at depth in the ocean, and blood pumping through arteries. Each context has evolved its own preferred pressure unit, which is why this converter is useful.

### The Six Units Supported

**Pascal (Pa)** is the SI base unit of pressure, defined as one newton of force per square meter of area. Individual Pascals are very small — atmospheric pressure at sea level is about 101,325 Pa. For this reason, kilopascals (kPa) are more practical: 1 kPa = 1,000 Pa. Standard atmosphere = 101.325 kPa.

**Bar** is a metric unit equal to exactly 100,000 Pa. It's close to but not equal to 1 atmosphere (1 atm = 1.01325 bar). Bar is widely used in European industry, compressors, and hydraulic systems.

**PSI (pounds per square inch)** is the imperial pressure unit. One psi = 6,894.757 Pa. PSI dominates in the US for tire pressures, hydraulics, plumbing, and compressed gas. A car tire is typically inflated to 30-35 psi.

**Atmosphere (atm)** is a reference unit equal to exactly 101,325 Pa — defined as the average air pressure at sea level. It's used as a baseline reference in chemistry, diving, and gas law calculations.

**Torr (mmHg)** equals 133.322 Pa. Historically defined as the pressure of 1 mm of mercury, it remains standard in vacuum science, blood pressure measurements (a normal reading is 120/80 mmHg), and laboratory instruments.

### Gauge Pressure vs. Absolute Pressure

This is an important distinction that the converter itself does not handle automatically.

**Absolute pressure** is measured from a perfect vacuum (zero pressure). **Gauge pressure** is measured relative to atmospheric pressure — a gauge that reads 0 psi when open to air is measuring gauge pressure.

When you inflate a car tire to "35 psi," your gauge is reading gauge pressure. The absolute pressure inside the tire is actually 35 + 14.7 = 49.7 psi absolute (adding 1 atmosphere). In engineering datasheets, pressures are sometimes labeled "psig" (gauge) and "psia" (absolute) to make this explicit.

This converter translates numerical values between pressure units. It does not automatically account for the 1 atm offset between gauge and absolute. If you need to convert gauge pressure in one unit to absolute pressure in another, add 1 atm (or 14.696 psi or 1.01325 bar) first, then convert.

### Common Mistakes

The most frequent confusion is treating bar and psi as interchangeable: they're not. 2.5 bar ≠ 2.5 psi. A pressure spec of "2.5 bar" is 2.5 x 14.5038 = 36.26 psi. Another common error is confusing kPa and Pa — off by a factor of 1,000. Tire pressures are typically 200-300 kPa; blood pressure is around 16 kPa (120 mmHg) systolic.
