# Energy Converter – Joules, kWh, Calories, BTU & More

### Energy: The Capacity to Do Work

Energy is what allows things to happen — it moves objects, heats substances, powers devices, and fuels living organisms. All forms of energy, from the chemical energy in food to the electrical energy in your utility bill to the mechanical energy in a spinning flywheel, can be expressed in the same fundamental unit: the joule.

The joule is the SI unit of energy. One joule is the amount of energy needed to exert one newton of force over one meter of distance. It's a relatively small unit — lifting a 100-gram apple one meter requires about 1 joule. That's why practical energy quantities are usually expressed in kilojoules (1,000 J), megajoules (1,000,000 J), kilowatt-hours, or domain-specific units like calories and BTUs.

---

### Energy vs. Power: An Important Distinction

Power and energy are related but different. **Power** (measured in watts) is the rate at which energy is used or produced — how fast, right now. **Energy** (measured in joules, watt-hours, or kilowatt-hours) is the cumulative total over time.

A 1,000-watt microwave uses 1,000 joules every second it operates. Run it for 2 minutes (120 seconds) and it consumes 1,000 × 120 = 120,000 joules = 0.0333 kWh of energy. Your electricity bill charges you for energy (kWh), not power (watts). This distinction becomes important when comparing energy figures — a heat pump rated at 3,000 watts and a boiler rated at 30,000 BTU/hour are both expressing power, not energy, which requires a different kind of comparison.

---

### The Units and Their Domains

**Joules and kilojoules (J, kJ)** are the scientific standard. Physics textbooks, chemistry problems, and engineering calculations typically use joules. One kilojoule = 1,000 joules.

**Calories and kilocalories (cal, kcal)** come from chemistry and nutrition. One small calorie (cal) = 4.184 joules — the heat needed to warm 1 gram of water by 1°C. One kilocalorie (kcal) = 4,184 joules. Food labels always mean kilocalories when they say "Calories" (capital C). A 300-Calorie snack contains 300 kcal = 1,255,200 joules = 1,255 kJ.

**Watt-hours and kilowatt-hours (Wh, kWh)** are the electrical energy units. One kilowatt-hour = 3,600,000 joules. This is what your electricity meter measures. Running a 100-watt bulb for 10 hours uses 1 kWh.

**BTU (British Thermal Unit)** is the US standard for heating and cooling equipment. One BTU = 1,055.06 joules. A central air conditioner might be rated at 24,000 BTU/hour (2 tons). Gas furnaces, water heaters, and ovens are also rated in BTUs.

**Foot-pounds (ft-lb)** appear in mechanical engineering: engine torque, structural load calculations, and physical work in the US customary system. One ft-lb = 1.3558 joules.

### A Fourth Worked Example (A Full Day's Calorie Intake: 2,000 kcal)

A common reference daily intake is 2,000 kcal. Converting to electrical and metric scientific units:

1. Convert to joules: 2,000 × 4,184 = **8,368,000 J**
2. Convert to kWh: 8,368,000 / 3,600,000 = **2.324 kWh**
3. Convert to kJ: 8,368,000 / 1,000 = **8,368 kJ**

*Verification Result: 2,000 kcal = **2.324 kWh** = **8,368 kJ**. This means a full day's food energy intake is roughly equivalent to the electricity a typical space heater consumes in just over 2 hours.*

### Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

**Q1: What is the difference between a calorie and a kilocalorie?**

The chemistry calorie (lowercase "c") is a small unit: the amount of energy needed to raise 1 gram of water by 1°C. It equals 4.184 joules. A kilocalorie (kcal) is 1,000 of these chemistry calories. In nutrition and on food labels, "Calories" (capital C) always means kilocalories. So when a food label says a meal has 500 Calories, it means 500 kcal = 500,000 chemistry calories = 2,092,000 joules.

**Q2: What is the difference between watts and watt-hours?**

Watts (W) measure power — the rate at which energy is used or produced. Watt-hours (Wh) and kilowatt-hours (kWh) measure energy — the total amount consumed over time. A 100-watt light bulb uses 100 joules every second. Run it for 10 hours and it has consumed 100 × 10 = 1,000 watt-hours = 1 kWh. Your electricity bill is in kWh, not watts.

**Q3: What is a BTU and where is it used?**

A British Thermal Unit (BTU) is the amount of heat energy needed to raise one pound of liquid water by 1°F. It equals approximately 1,055 joules. BTUs are widely used in the US to rate air conditioners, furnaces, water heaters, and boilers. A 10,000 BTU air conditioner can remove 10,000 BTUs of heat per hour from a room.

**Q4: How many joules are in a kilowatt-hour?**

Exactly 3,600,000 joules (3.6 megajoules). One kilowatt = 1,000 watts = 1,000 joules per second. One hour = 3,600 seconds. So 1 kWh = 1,000 × 3,600 = 3,600,000 joules.

**Q5: What is a foot-pound in energy terms?**

A foot-pound (ft-lb) is the amount of energy required to lift one pound of force through one foot of distance. It equals approximately 1.3558 joules. Foot-pounds appear in mechanical engineering contexts, particularly for torque specifications and mechanical work in the US customary system.
